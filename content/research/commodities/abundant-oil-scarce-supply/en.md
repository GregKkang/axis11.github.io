*From resource scarcity to supply-route scarcity*

Understanding the long-term evolution of oil prices requires a distinction between oil in the ground and oil that can reach consumers. The shale revolution expanded the resource base that can be developed economically. Transport constraints, however, can limit how much of that oil reaches the market. Resources have become more abundant, while supply can still be scarce at a particular time and place.

**My base case is for lower oil prices over the medium term, conditional on a recovery in transport routes. Current physical disruptions can nevertheless justify the recent sharp price spikes.** The direction over the next six to twelve months may differ from the path over the next few weeks. The focus of this analysis is when the market begins to expect a shorter disruption. If stronger incentives to negotiate and adjustments in supply and demand change that expectation, prices can start falling before oil flows normalise.

## 1. How shale changed the economics of oil supply

In the early to mid-2000s, rapidly growing demand from emerging economies exposed the limits of oil supply. Production struggled to respond quickly enough, driving prices higher and raising concerns about whether the industry could keep pace with continued demand growth[^fed2008]. The prospect of persistently scarce oil was central to the market's outlook.

The shale revolution changed that calculation. Horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing made previously uneconomic resources commercially accessible, expanding the pool of oil that producers could develop when prices justified the investment. **Shale expanded the supply response to higher prices, strengthening the mechanism through which those prices could eventually be restrained.** Shale therefore shifted attention from the availability of oil underground towards the cost and speed of bringing it into production[^dallas2019].

That change became visible in the 2014–2016 downturn. Growing US shale output helped loosen the supply balance, while changes in OPEC policy, easing geopolitical concerns and a weaker demand outlook reinforced the decline[^wb2018]. Annual average Brent prices fell from $108.56 in 2013 to $43.64 in 2016[^brent]. Shale was one of several drivers, but the episode demonstrated how an expanding supply base could undermine expectations of sustained high prices.

The strength of this mechanism depends on time. Producers must commit capital, secure equipment, drill wells and connect new output to transport infrastructure. A June 2026 Dallas Fed analysis estimated that production initiated in response to higher prices could take six to eight months to reach major pipelines, with capital discipline, equipment shortages and constraints on transporting associated natural gas limiting the response[^dallas2026].

**Shale strengthened the market's ability to respond to high prices over the medium term; it did not make additional supply immediately available.** This distinction helps explain why abundant resources can restrain prices over time while offering little immediate relief from a sudden disruption.

## 2. How oil markets adjust to supply shocks

A war can continue even as its impact on the oil market fades. Lost supply may be restored or replaced, trade can find alternative routes, and high prices can weaken demand. As these adjustments become more credible, the market can begin to anticipate a less severe or shorter-lived shortage, allowing prices to fall before the conflict ends. Historical episodes show how this process can unfold—and why it sometimes takes much longer.

**1990–1991: A sharp shock followed by a reversal.** Brent more than doubled within 90 days of Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, but returned close to its pre-invasion level within a year[^brent]. The episode shows that a sudden supply shock need not establish a permanently higher price level. It also provides a useful reminder that oil markets could normalise well before shale expanded their capacity to respond.

**2019: Rapid repairs contained the disruption.** The attacks on Saudi Arabia's Abqaiq and Khurais facilities caused an immediate price jump, but Brent had returned close to its pre-attack level by the end of September. Aramco reported restoring production levels within 11 days[^brent][^aramco]. The key was the speed of recovery: a large initial loss proved short-lived. Prices rose again towards year-end, showing that resolving a specific disruption removes one source of pressure without determining the market's subsequent direction.

**2022: Trade adapted while the war continued.** Russia's invasion of Ukraine raised the prospect of a prolonged loss of Russian supply. In practice, exports were redirected to other buyers, while additional non-OPEC production and changes in demand helped the market adjust[^wb2024]. Brent fell substantially from its initial peak and stood below its pre-invasion level a year later, even as the war continued[^brent]. The market's adjustment depended on how much oil remained available globally, including through reorganised trade.

**2011: Limited supply flexibility prolonged the pressure.** Libya provides a counterexample to rapid normalisation. Brent remained above its pre-disruption level a year after the unrest began, and annual average prices exceeded $100 throughout 2011–2013[^brent]. In an environment with limited spare supply and recurring disruptions, adjustments can be too slow to relieve the pressure. An initial price spike is therefore insufficient grounds for expecting a sustained decline.

These episodes show that **prices can normalise through different forms of adjustment.** Saudi Arabia restored production quickly in 2019, while the market adapted after 2022 through redirected Russian exports, additional supply elsewhere and changes in demand. The relevant question for the current conflict is which adjustments can offset the disruption and how quickly.

The constraint at Hormuz is physical: finding a new buyer does little for a cargo that cannot leave the Gulf. Recovery therefore depends on some combination of restored passage, alternative transport routes, replacement supply and weaker demand. My medium-term bearish view rests on these adjustments gradually outweighing further supply losses. 

## 3. The current market: transport scarcity

The latest FRED observation used in this note is **9 September 2026**, when Brent spot was **$109.51** and WTI spot was **$97.26**[^brent][^wti]. These are spot benchmarks. Their dates, instruments and delivery terms differ from the front-month futures prices quoted in subsequent news reports.

Reuters reporting on 14 September described the shutdown of Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, attacks on vessels in the strait and rising risks around Bab el-Mandeb. The planned meeting in Oman had also been postponed[^reuters-oil]. In these circumstances, the existence of a bypass pipeline cannot be treated as available supply. Nameplate capacity, actual utilisation, port loadings and deliveries to destinations are separate measures.

Current oil prices reflect both the shortage of available barrels and concern over how much worse the disruption could become. That concern can encourage precautionary stockbuilding and speculative buying, adding to the pressure from immediate physical demand. These effects are difficult to separate, but they need not unwind at the same time.

Physical supply may remain tight even as confidence in an eventual recovery improves. If the market begins to expect a shorter disruption, some of the upward pressure on prices can ease before additional barrels arrive. This makes the changing incentives to negotiate relevant to the price outlook: a growing willingness to compromise can alter expectations well before it produces an agreement.

## 4. Negotiating intent beneath the rhetoric

The United States and Iran face different cost curves as the conflict continues, and they perceive different benefits from negotiation. Elections, domestic politics, fiscal burdens and military constraints change their incentives to negotiate at different speeds. Tougher rhetoric can coexist with a greater willingness to reach an agreement. Escalating pressure to secure better terms can be part of preparing for a compromise.

**Stronger rhetoric alone is insufficient evidence that an agreement has become less likely.** What matters is how pressure changes each side's calculation. Confrontation may continue while further pressure appears rewarding. But if the costs of waiting rise faster than the expected gains from holding out, the incentive to accelerate negotiations can strengthen even as the public stance remains uncompromising.

These calculations extend beyond Washington and Tehran. Reuters reported that Houthi threats to Red Sea shipping, combined with disruption in Hormuz, were increasing the need for some Gulf states to pursue their own diplomacy with Iran rather than rely solely on the United States[^reuters-gulf]. This suggests that escalation can strengthen Iran's bargaining position while also increasing pressure for compromise from neighbouring countries bearing the costs. Even without a comprehensive US–Iran settlement, those states have an incentive to pursue limited arrangements that protect shipping and their economic interests.

A greater need for negotiations does not guarantee immediate progress. The postponement of Gulf–Iran talks and Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia illustrate how escalation can obstruct diplomatic engagement. Yet a near-term setback in talks does not necessarily mean the incentive to compromise has weakened. The cost of sustaining the confrontation can rise as public hostility intensifies.

Oil prices may turn before an agreement is announced, when the market perceives that the parties increasingly see compromise as more advantageous than holding out. Even if current disruptions worsen, credible signs of a greater willingness to compromise can shorten the market's expected duration of the shortage. The war risk premium could then contract before physical flows recover.

This is the possibility at the centre of the analysis: the point at which expectations that pressure will bring an earlier compromise outweigh fears of further supply disruption. Paradoxically, the most confrontational phase could coincide with a turn in prices as the market begins to anticipate progress towards an agreement.

## 5. The outlook: medium-term normalisation, near-term instability

Over the next six to twelve months, I expect additional supply, softer demand and a gradual recovery in transport routes to put downward pressure on oil prices. This outlook assumes that some oil flows can resume while the conflict continues, and that further infrastructure damage or producer cuts do not fully offset the improvement. 

| Path | Developments that would change expectations | Expected price response |
|---|---|---|
| Gradual normalisation · base case | Limited diplomatic progress; expanding alternative flows; more credible recovery prospects | The premium for prolonged disruption contracts before full normalisation, followed by additional downward pressure from supply and demand adjustment |
| Prolonged disruption · principal upside risk | Persistent disruption in both Hormuz and the Red Sea, with diplomatic deadlock extending the expected duration | Sustained high prices or further spikes as supply losses outweigh weaker demand |
| Rapid reassessment of diplomacy · faster decline | Credible signs that the parties favour compromise; acceptance of mediation proposals and more flexible demands | A rapid contraction in the risk premium before an agreement is announced or physical flows recover |

High prices may leave a lasting mark on demand even after transport routes recover. Some consumption will return as prices fall, but efficiency improvements and shifts to alternative technologies can persist. The World Bank's 2025 analysis identified electric-vehicle adoption as one factor restraining oil demand[^wb2025]. Over the next six to twelve months, reduced consumption and a weaker economy may have a more immediate effect.

The implication is that returning supply could meet a softer demand environment than was expected before the disruption. If that happens, restoring oil flows would do more than ease the current shortage: it could expose a weaker underlying balance and put further downward pressure on prices. OPEC+ could cushion that adjustment through production cuts.

For this reason, a return to the $60s should not be treated as a predetermined outcome. Brent spot averaged $69.14 per barrel in 2025, but that provides historical context rather than a price target. The level at which prices settle will depend on how much supply returns, how much demand recovers and how producers respond.

**Nor does the bearish case require one final price spike.** Prices can begin to fall as the market gains confidence in a shorter disruption or a softer balance ahead. A shift towards negotiation could bring that reassessment forward, even while physical supply remains tight. Waiting for a final surge therefore risks missing a turning point that may emerge when supply anxiety still appears most intense.

## 6. What to monitor

The following indicators provide a framework for testing the medium-term bearish scenario. Negotiating incentives and market reactions may signal a price turn; actual transport volumes provide supporting evidence of progress towards normalisation.

| Indicator | Supports the medium-term bearish view | Strengthens upside risk |
|---|---|---|
| Negotiating incentives and terms | More flexible demands despite tough rhetoric; concrete efforts by neighbouring states to secure limited agreements | Hardening demands as further pressure is seen as rewarding; rejection of mediation and closure of negotiating channels |
| Price response to adverse supply news | Repeatedly smaller rallies after new attacks or disruptions, with gains quickly reversed | Larger and more persistent rallies following adverse news of comparable severity |
| Physical differentials and the futures curve | Easing physical premiums, or falling deferred prices despite continued nearby tightness as the market anticipates normalisation | Strength in spot and nearby prices spreading into deferred contracts as prolonged shortages are priced in |
| End demand and product inventories | Weaker product consumption and refining margins alongside rising product inventories | Resilient consumption despite high prices, with continuing inventory draws |
| Non-Middle Eastern supply and OPEC+ | Increasingly credible production and export growth outside the Middle East, with limited prospects of offsetting OPEC+ cuts | Retreat from expansion plans, export constraints or a stronger willingness to offset recovering supply through cuts |
| Transport recovery · supporting evidence | Sustained improvement in pipelines, ports and straits, supporting recovery expectations | Repeated attacks and disruption to alternative routes extending expected repair and recovery times |

The futures curve helps distinguish immediate scarcity from expectations of a prolonged shortage. Backwardation—when nearby oil is more expensive than oil for later delivery—can deepen even as the medium-term outlook softens, if deferred prices fall while nearby prices remain elevated. A wider gap between nearby and deferred futures prices therefore does not necessarily signal a more bullish outlook: it may reflect falling deferred prices rather than rising nearby prices.

These signals are more useful when they reinforce one another. Smaller rallies on adverse supply news may simply reflect positioning or news already absorbed by the market. But if they coincide with more flexible negotiating terms, falling deferred prices and weaker consumption, the evidence for easing medium-term pressure becomes stronger, even while physical supply remains tight.

The opposite combination would challenge the bearish case. Rising deferred prices, limited supply growth and resilient demand would suggest that the shortage is becoming more persistent. Repeated infrastructure damage or producer cuts that offset recovering supply would provide further reasons to reassess the outlook.

My medium-term bearish view rests on supply growth, trade adjustment and softer demand gradually outweighing further disruption. High prices can accelerate those adjustments while increasing the incentive to compromise. Prices may turn when the market begins to see these responses gaining ground, well before the war ends or physical shortages disappear.
