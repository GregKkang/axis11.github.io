*The relief of normalising logistics, and the new supply shock coming from the weather*

World grain production this year is historically high. Grain prices are rising anyway, because the crop cannot move. Once shipping normalises and the trapped volume reaches the market, prices can settle. That path lowers the pressure on food prices and helps inflation come down.

But this year's El Niño is on a record scale. World cereal production is not collapsing, yet the effect on the harvests still to come has not played out. Warming adds a second question on top of it: how much production risk sits at 2°C.

**Just as the logistics bottleneck clears and prices are expected to settle, can a worsening harvest produce a fresh round of food price increases?** This note looks at how that risk could unsettle the inflation outlook for the global economy and for asset markets.

## 1. Production is high and prices are rising

The FAO's 2 October forecast puts 2026 world cereal production at **2,979 million tonnes**. That is 2.1% below last year's record but still the second highest ever recorded. The projected 2026/27 stocks-to-use ratio of **31.7%** likewise shows no immediate shortage in the global aggregate.[^1]

Yet the FAO cereal price index reached **122.8** in September, up **5.1%** on the month and **17.2%** on the year. Wheat and maize both rose: despite the high harvest, the grain market is pricing supply anxiety.[^2]

The FAO lowered its 2026/27 cereal trade forecast to reflect Black Sea shipping constraints and the shortage of alternative capacity. At the same time it raised its forecast for Russian and Ukrainian wheat stocks, reflecting lower exports. Grain that has been produced is staying in the exporting country, and what is available to the international market shrinks.[^1]

Those Black Sea disruptions were the main force behind September's wheat rally. Maize was driven by lower-than-expected US yields and reduced Brazilian export availability. Concerns over fuel, fertiliser and freight costs supported prices further.[^2]

If logistics improve, stocks stranded in exporting countries reach the market and the cost of sourcing alternatives falls. That is a reasonable path to lower prices. But normalising transport is a question of moving grain that already exists; the size of the next harvest is a separate question. **If the crop deteriorates while the logistics clear, the supply recovery may be smaller than expected.**

## 2. Is a record El Niño a risk to the next harvest?

On 3 September the WMO said El Niño would strengthen further towards the end of the year, with a **near-100% probability of persisting through February 2027**. On 22 September the UK Met Office reported that observed sea surface temperatures in the central equatorial Pacific had exceeded the records set in previous events. This year's El Niño has moved past being forecast as exceptional: on that measure it has already broken the record.[^3][^4]

The global production forecast remains high, so no broad supply risk has been confirmed. Even so, heat, dryness and downgraded crop prospects have already been reported in some regions, which makes "nothing has happened yet" hard to write. The abundance of current production and the scope for damage to widen are two different things.[^1][^2]

What matters for agriculture is which growing and harvesting calendars this intensity overlaps with. The Met Office pointed to reduced rainfall across the Indian monsoon region, the western Pacific and parts of Latin America, and to drought risk in southern Africa. The WMO notes that regional outcomes will depend on conditions in the Indian Ocean and the Atlantic.[^3][^4]

The market should watch the following channels. This is a priority list for monitoring the crop, not a damage forecast for this year.

| Region and crop | Risk to watch | Indicator to connect to the production forecast |
|---|---|---|
| Rice in India and Southeast Asia | Rainfall deficit and irrigation constraints | Rainfall, reservoir levels, planted area and yield forecasts |
| Wheat in Australia | Moisture shortage late in the growing season | Soil moisture, rainfall during grain fill, state-level yield forecasts |
| Maize in southern Africa | Drought during planting and growth | Planting progress, rainfall, crop condition |
| Maize and soybeans in South America | Regional drought or excess rain | Planting delays, crop condition, regional forecast revisions |

If El Niño persists from the end of this year into early next, it overlaps with the southern hemisphere's next crop cycle. This year's high world production is therefore not by itself a reason to be comfortable about supply from 2026/27 onwards. But a record sea surface temperature does not mean a record production loss. The scale of the damage depends on actual rainfall and heat, and on the growth stage they hit.

## 3. What do ENSO and rising temperatures do to production?

### El Niño and La Niña: the effect depends on the crop and the region

The **2014 Nature Communications study by Iizumi and colleagues** used 1982–2006 data to analyse the relationship between El Niño, La Niña and yields of maize, rice, wheat and soybeans. In El Niño years, global average soybean yields were **+2.1% to +5.4%**, while maize, rice and wheat ranged from **−4.3% to +0.8%** depending on the crop and the estimate. In La Niña years all four crops tended to come in below normal, with estimates spanning **−4.5% to 0.0%**. Each range groups results across crops; it does not mean the same reduction applies to every crop.[^5]

The positive global result for soybeans under El Niño matters, because drought in one region can be offset by better rainfall in another. La Niña is the reverse case: global average yields for all four crops studied tended to fall.

The **2018 Nature Communications study by Heino and colleagues** examined ENSO, the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) against simulated crop productivity for 1961–2010. About **two-thirds** of global cropland showed a significant relationship with at least one of the three oscillations, but explanatory power at the global aggregate was low, because opposing regional effects cancel out.[^6]

Past averages cannot simply be applied to an event of unusual intensity. The research is a basis for selecting which regions to watch; the scale of this year's losses has to be confirmed against actual rainfall, soil moisture and yield forecasts.

### Rising average temperature: the negative effect on yields is more consistent

The **2017 PNAS study by Zhao and colleagues** combined results from four methods — crop models, statistical analysis and field warming experiments among them. Excluding CO₂ fertilisation, effective adaptation and genetic improvement, a 1°C rise in global mean temperature was estimated to change average yields as follows.[^7]

| Crop | Yield change per 1°C of global mean warming |
|---|---:|
| Maize | −7.4% |
| Wheat | −6.0% |
| Rice | −3.2% |
| Soybeans | −3.1% |

These are sensitivities to temperature, not a forecast of this year's losses. Warming that has already occurred cannot be multiplied back onto this year's production, and the percentages cannot be converted into price increases.

Damage already incurred has also been studied. The **2011 Science study by Lobell and colleagues** estimated that climate trends over 1980–2008 left maize production **3.8%** and wheat production **5.5%** lower. The comparison is with production in the absence of those trends — not a claim that output fell by that much against the past. It shows that climate can erode part of the gain even while production is rising.[^8]

More recent work accounts for adaptation. The **2025 Nature study by Hultgren and colleagues** found that even after allowing for observed farm-level adaptation and income changes, global average yields for the crops studied — rice excepted — are likely to be lower by 2050 under a high-emissions scenario than they would be without climate change. The result rests on the current distribution of cropland, and changes with the treatment of cropland migration, crop switching and CO₂ effects.[^9]

### From 1.5°C to 2°C: the risk of several breadbaskets failing at once

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2022) judged that crop production losses could be substantial even at 1.5°C of warming above pre-industrial levels. West African maize yields were projected to fall about 9% against 2005 without adaptation, and above 2°C the loss in the same region could reach 20–40% even allowing for adaptation and CO₂ fertilisation. The two projections rest on different assumptions about adaptation and CO₂, so the difference between them cannot be read as the effect of the additional 0.5°C alone.[^10] In Europe, maize yields were assessed to fall 10–25% at 1.5–2°C of warming.[^16] Losses of this kind cannot be recovered by fixing logistics. If damage on this scale spreads across major supplying regions, stable grain prices and easing food inflation become much harder to achieve.

As warming moves from 1.5°C to 2°C, the risk that several major producing regions are hit in the same year rises sharply. In the **2019 Agricultural Systems study by Gaupp and colleagues**, the probability that all five major maize-producing regions experience climate conditions associated with a poor harvest in the same year rose from 6.1% in the baseline scenario (2006–2015, about 0.85°C above pre-industrial) to 39.0% at 1.5°C and 53.8% at 2°C. For wheat the sequence was 2.3% → 4.7% → 6.6%, and for soybeans 4.9% → 11.6% → 14.3%. The conditions in question are those associated with the bottom quartile of detrended yields; they do not mean yields fall by 25%. The point is that the scope for one region's shortfall to be covered by another shrinks.[^11] When several supplying regions are hit together, a shortage can appear even with logistics working normally, pushing food inflation back up.

If long-run warming moves from 1.5°C to 2°C above pre-industrial levels, production risk in agriculture can widen with it. What decides a harvest is the heat and moisture during the actual growing season, and the research supports the view that unfavourable conditions — and the risk of simultaneous shocks — become more likely as warming proceeds.

## 4. The risk that a production shortfall follows the logistics shortfall

A logistics disruption is a problem of grain that exists but cannot reach the market. Restart the transport and supply can recover. A poor harvest is a problem of the next crop being smaller. Repairing the shipping route cannot bring back a harvest that was never produced.

Today's high production and stocks provide room to absorb a shock. But if shortfalls repeat in major supplying regions, or several exporters are hit at once, stocks become hard to rebuild. In that case grain prices may settle temporarily as logistics normalise, only for the next season's shortage to push them up again.

The risk does not stop at grain prices. Grain is the input for processed food and the feed for livestock. If higher input and feed costs pass through to processed food, meat and dairy, the pressure on food prices broadens. How fast and how far depends on corporate margins, contracts, exchange rates and distribution costs.

What this note is concerned with, then, is not high grain prices persisting as such, but **the risk that repeated increases in food prices unsettle the path back to stable inflation**. A single step up in prices that then stops has an effect on the inflation rate that fades with time. If production shocks repeat and prices keep rising, the burden lasts much longer.

## 5. From grain to food as a whole: how far is the pressure spreading?

The rise in international prices is spreading beyond grain. The FAO food price index rose 5.8% year on year in September. Cereals were up 17.2%, vegetable oils 18.3% and sugar 14.7%, all far above the overall rate. Meat was in line with a year earlier and dairy fell 19.1%. The items driving food prices are concentrated in field crops.[^2]

| Item | September price move | What supported the recent increase |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat | +6.3% on the month, highest since August 2023 | Black Sea logistics constraints, dry weather in North America at planting |
| Maize | +5.6% on the month, highest in more than three years | Lower-than-expected US yields, reduced Brazilian export availability |
| Palm oil | Fourth consecutive monthly rise | Import demand and production concerns from dry weather in Southeast Asia |
| Sugar | Third consecutive monthly rise | Lower Thai output forecasts, rainfall deficits in India and El Niño, delayed Brazilian harvest |

*Table source: FAO, commentary on the September 2026 Food Price Index.[^2]*

This spread across items shows that normalising logistics may not relieve all of the pressure on food prices. Even if the Black Sea problem eases for wheat, poor weather in sugar and palm oil producing regions leaves the cost of other food inputs elevated.[^2]

Pressure on consumer food prices is sharper in vulnerable countries. According to the World Bank's September report, food inflation exceeded headline inflation in about 22% of the 150 countries analysed in the latest quarterly data, up from 14% in the previous report. Pressure was highest in Eastern and Southern Africa, the Middle East and North Africa, and South and Central Asia. Nigeria's food inflation reached 20.3% in July, and wheat flour prices in Pakistan rose more than 73% year on year. Even when global supply in aggregate is sufficient, the burden on food prices varies enormously with domestic transport, distribution and production conditions.[^13]

In the United States, the rise in international prices has not yet shown up as a jump in grocery inflation. In August, food prices rose 2.7% year on year, food at home 2.2% and food away from home 3.4%. Food at home was unchanged on the month. By item, however, nonalcoholic beverages rose 3.7% year on year, fruit and vegetables 3.2% and cereals and bakery products 2.6%, while dairy fell 0.3%. If international agricultural prices keep rising, whether this stability in grocery inflation holds becomes the question.[^14]

The food industry is flagging cost pressure running into next year. In its September outlook, the UK's Food and Drink Federation (FDF) expected food and non-alcoholic drink inflation to reach 3.9% by the end of 2026 and 6.4% in July 2027, reflecting the cost burden from the Iran conflict together with El Niño and this summer's droughts in the UK and Europe. Manufacturers absorbed the initial cost shock, the FDF says, but the accumulated burden can show up in consumer prices with a lag. It is an industry forecast rather than an observation, but it illustrates how long a cost shock in production and transport can stay in food prices.[^15]

Rising food prices feed directly into consumer price inflation. The IMF's work on international food prices and domestic inflation finds that the pass-through depends on the share of food in consumption and on how well anchored inflation expectations are. If repeated shocks start to affect wage and price setting, the burden on monetary policy grows.[^12] **A central bank cannot improve a harvest with interest rates.** But if price increases persist and expectations drift up with them, it may have to delay cutting rates even as growth slows. That is the path on which asset markets face weaker growth and price pressure at the same time.

## Conclusion

This year's high grain production is a foundation for price stability. As logistics normalise, grain supply can recover and the pressure on food prices can ease with it. But if a record El Niño damages the harvests still to come, and warming raises the risk of simultaneous failures across major producing regions, that stability may not last.

**The core risk is that a production problem emerges once the logistics problem is solved, and food inflation spreads a second time.** That weakens household purchasing power and corporate earnings while making monetary easing harder. For a global economy and asset markets already sensitive to inflation, it is an additional burden. The conclusion is not that a broad supply collapse has been confirmed — it is that high production alone does not settle the question of food price risk ahead.
