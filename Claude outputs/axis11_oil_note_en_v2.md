*From resource scarcity to supply-route scarcity*

Understanding the long-run shift in the oil market requires separating the oil that exists underground from the oil that can reach a consumer. The shale revolution widened the range of resources that are economic to develop. But the existence of a field does not substitute for a blockaded strait or a destroyed pipeline. Resources have become abundant; supply, at a particular time and in a particular place, can still fall short.

**My base case is a medium-term decline in oil prices, conditional on transport normalising. At the same time, the physical disruption now in place is more than enough to justify the short-term spike.** The starting point of this note is that the direction over the next six to twelve months and the price path over the next few weeks can point in opposite directions. What matters is not calling the end date of a war, but observing how much oil actually returns to the market and how quickly.

## 1. From depletion fears to the economics of supply

One of the dominant concerns in the oil market of the early-to-mid 2000s was whether production could keep pace with demand growth. Rapid growth in emerging economies and short-run supply constraints supported higher prices. The Federal Reserve's retrospective on 2008 also points to emerging-market demand and limited supply as the backdrop to that year's first-half surge[^fed2008]. Explaining the episode purely as speculation or depletion fear is not sufficient.

Horizontal drilling combined with hydraulic fracturing subsequently brought oil that had been uneconomic into the supply base. The essence of the shale revolution is not that oil became limitless, but that **a change in prices and technology sharply increased the volume of resources that could additionally be produced**. The Dallas Fed has described the mechanism by which shale flattens the supply-cost curve and constrains long-run prices[^dallas2019].

The period in which this change became unmistakable in prices was 2014–2016. The World Bank attributed the decline to expanding US shale output, a shift in OPEC policy and easing geopolitical concerns, followed by a deteriorating demand outlook[^wb2018]. Calculated from FRED daily data, the annual average Brent price fell from $108.56 in 2013 to $43.64 in 2016[^brent].

A lag between the market and the supply response nevertheless remains. According to the Dallas Fed's June 2026 analysis, new production responding to a price signal can take six to eight months to reach a major pipeline. Capital discipline, take-away constraints on associated natural gas, and equipment shortages also limit the increase[^dallas2026]. **Technology raised the responsiveness of medium-term supply; it did not establish a firm ceiling on short-term prices.**

## 2. Prices can normalise while a war continues

Oil prices respond less to the existence of a war than to the change that war produces in actual supply and demand. Where production is restored, inventories are drawn down, trade routes are redirected and demand softens, prices can fall before the war ends. The speed of normalisation, however, is not the same across shocks.

**1990–91: a supply shock, and the resolution of uncertainty.** Immediately before Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, Brent stood at $19.93. The highest price within the following 90 days was $41.45, yet one year after the event the price was $19.40. Because price recovery appeared even before shale, this pattern cannot be treated as an exclusive consequence of the shale revolution[^brent].

**2019: a case in which the speed of repair set the price.** Brent spot stood at $61.25 immediately before the strike on Saudi Arabia's Abqaiq and Khurais facilities, rose to $68.42 on 16 September, and was back to $60.99 by 30 September. Aramco stated that it restored production to prior levels within eleven days[^brent][^aramco]. The duration of the loss mattered as much as its initial size. Prices did rise again into year-end, so the resolution of the initial shock did not imply a sustained decline thereafter.

**2022: a price adjustment that proceeded without the war ending.** Brent stood at $99.29 immediately before the Russian invasion and reached $133.18 on 8 March. One year later it was $82.31. Annual averages also fell, from $100.93 in 2022 to $82.49 in 2023 and $69.14 in 2025[^brent]. The World Bank describes the redirection of Russian exports to other markets, expanding non-OPEC supply including the United States, and demand conditions[^wb2024]. The scenario in which Russian crude disappeared entirely from world markets differed from the outcome of the actual trade reconfiguration.

**2011: the counter-example, where normalisation took a long time.** Measured from 15 February, early in the Libyan crisis, Brent rose from $103.12 immediately prior to $126.64 within 90 days, and was still at $120.25 one year later. Annual averages for 2011–2013 all exceeded $100[^brent]. Where spare capacity is thin and shocks repeat, risk premia and physical shortage can persist. A simple "buy the war spike, then sell" rule does not hold.

The shared lesson across these cases is that **normalisation requires a working channel**. In 2019 the facility was repaired quickly; after 2022 there was trade reconfiguration alongside supply and demand adjustment. Oil that cannot pass through Hormuz today is not like Russian crude, which could move simply by changing destination. A situation in which the route itself is closed requires a different remedy.

## 3. The market today: transport scarcity rather than resource scarcity

The latest FRED observation in this note is **9 September 2026**. On that date Brent spot stood at **$109.51** and WTI spot at **$97.26**[^brent][^wti]. Both are spot benchmarks, and differ from the front-month futures quoted in subsequent reporting in date, contract and delivery terms.

Reuters reporting on 14 September confirmed the shutdown of the Saudi East-West pipeline, vessels struck in the strait, and widening risk around Bab el-Mandeb. A scheduled meeting in Oman was also postponed[^reuters-oil]. In such conditions the existence of a bypass pipeline cannot be counted directly as available supply. Nameplate capacity, actual utilisation, port loadings and arrivals at destination are separate quantities.

Beyond the actual shortfall in supply, prices can also reflect anxiety about further disruption, precautionary inventory building and positioning in financial markets. Spot and futures prices alone, however, cannot separate these contributions. There is not yet a sufficient basis either to assert that most of the current increase is speculative, or to subtract a specific dollar amount as a war premium and compute a fair price.

Strengthening the case for a decline requires seeing production and loadings recover, the pace of inventory drawdown slow, freight and insurance costs fall, and the relative strength of front-month contracts ease. That front-month strength — backwardation — is the condition in which oil for near-term delivery is more expensive than oil to be received later.

## 4. Negotiation signals: workable agreements matter more than rhetoric

The central point at this stage was that the United States and Iran bear different costs as time passes. Elections, domestic politics, and fiscal and military constraints give each side different incentives to negotiate. A strategy of raising pressure while preparing to negotiate is available, and hard rhetoric and a willingness to negotiate do not necessarily move in opposite directions.

**Stronger rhetoric alone is not sufficient grounds for lowering the probability of a settlement.** The same statement may be a negotiating tactic or a genuine intent to escalate. The US midterm elections make mid-September a politically significant window to watch, but I do not set it as a deadline by which either side must improve its bargaining position. That is a political hypothesis, not a publicly verified schedule.

What will decide the direction of oil prices is the point at which the cost of sustaining the war and the incentive to compromise intersect for the United States and Iran. That balance, however, is not settled by those two countries alone. As time passes, more countries carry the political and economic burden of disrupted transport and high prices, and their interests bear on the negotiation too. Reuters reported that the additional threat to shipping routes in the Red Sea is increasing the need for independent diplomacy by the Gulf states[^reuters-gulf]. This suggests that even before the American and Iranian cost curves intersect directly, mediation and pressure from neighbouring states could produce an agreement on navigation safety or on limited oil transit.

## 5. The path ahead: medium-term normalisation, short-term instability

If supply routes are progressively restored over the next six to twelve months, I treat the persistence of lower prices than today's as the base scenario. This is not a probabilistic forecast from a statistical model but **a conditional judgement resting on transport restoration, the supply response and demand adjustment**. For as long as both sides keep raising the cost, the point at which this view is realised can keep being pushed back.

| Path | Required conditions | Expected price response | Signal to change the view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gradual normalisation · base | Pipeline repair, improved navigation safety, rising loadings | Downward pressure over 6–12 months after near-term volatility | Inventory drawdown and spot strength persist even as volumes recover |
| Prolonged disruption · main upside risk | Hormuz and Red Sea disruption persisting together, facilities struck again | Higher prices for longer, with further spikes possible | Stable volume recovery and falling transport costs |
| Rapid transit agreement · faster decline | A workable agreement and an actual recovery in volumes | Risk premium can compress quickly, ahead of physical improvement | Transit, insurance and loadings fail to normalise even after an agreement |

I do not adopt the $60s of the past as an automatic price target. The 2025 Brent annual average of $69.14 is a historical reference, not an estimate of the equilibrium price for 2026–2027. Current production costs, the OPEC+ response, inventories and transport costs can all differ. **For the same reason, I do not consider the timing or the peak of a final spike to be precisely predictable.**

On the demand side, the channel by which high prices suppress consumption and accelerate adoption of substitutes has to be considered. The World Bank's 2025 analysis identified electric-vehicle adoption as a factor constraining growth in oil demand[^wb2025]. Electrifying road transport does not, however, simultaneously remove aviation, marine and petrochemical demand. Wider EV adoption is an argument for a lower medium-term demand growth rate; it is not a factor that resolves the present physical shortfall.

## 6. Five indicators that would change the view

| What to watch | Strengthens the medium-term decline | Strengthens the upside risk |
|---|---|---|
| East-West pipeline and Yanbu loadings | Sustained recovery in actual throughput and loadings | Delayed repair, renewed strikes, restricted port access |
| Hormuz and Red Sea navigation | Rising tanker transits and falling insurance costs | Falling transits, more vessel damage and insurance limits |
| Physical prices and the futures curve | Easing spot premium and backwardation | Widening spot premium and front-month strength |
| Inventories and end consumption | Slower drawdown, weaker consumption and refinery demand | Sharp drawdown coexisting with firm consumption |
| Non–Middle East supply and OPEC+ | Net output growth and export expansion confirmed in volumes | Cuts or production constraints offsetting restored volumes |

Rather than a blanket assumption that alternative routes and new supply "resolve it within a few months," the oil price outlook needs to be updated as these indicators actually change. In particular, if the bypass routes are themselves attacked, the medium-term logic of abundant resources is no reason to disregard a short-term shortfall.

That said, the reason to expect medium-term normalisation in oil prices is not that the risk of war is small. It is that high prices can induce a supply response, trade reconfiguration and demand adjustment. **The end date of a war cannot be forecast. But the fact that oil is an abundant resource does not change.**

## Data and interpretation

- Reference date: 14 September 2026. Latest observation retrieved from the FRED API: 9 September 2026. News is verified through public reporting on 14 September.
- Series: DCOILBRENTEU, DCOILWTICO. The originating agency is the U.S. Energy Information Administration; the distribution platform is FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Daily, not seasonally adjusted, nominal USD per barrel. The latest IEA and EIA supply-demand outlooks are not used.
- Missing values are preserved. Monthly and annual averages are arithmetic means of the valid daily observations in the period, neither interpolated nor carried forward. Chart lines connect valid observations. Partial months are distinguished from complete ones.
- Event comparisons use the same Brent spot series. Because confounders exist — the 2008 and 2020 demand shocks, prior anticipation, OPEC policy — the charts alone are not used to estimate the independent effect of conflict or of shale.
- Current and historical prices are kept separate from the outlook. Price targets and scenario probabilities are not estimated quantitatively. Institutional forecasts cited in the text are analyses made at the time and are not reused as current forecasts.
